The effort ledger under a thousand-hour year

A thousand working hours a year is the real budget, and most plans spend it before the calendar opens. A course at $997 and ten hours a week says both numbers out loud: the fee hits the card once, the 520 hours come out of evenings for a year. Price those hours at $25, $13,000 in effort goes into the project before a dollar comes back, and a market index compounding at 10 percent turns the same $13,000 spread across 35 years into about $3.5 million. At five hours a week the effort halves and the compounding still lands near $1.8 million, so the spread between the two plans is mostly hours, and hours are the part nobody prices.

The 4 percent rule prices freedom in hours. $40,000 a year of spending at 4 percent takes a $1 million portfolio, and drawn across a 35-year retirement that million buys waking hours at about $4.90 each: 16 waking hours a day for 35 years is about 204,000 hours. Hours that sold at $25 while you earned them come back at $4.90 once you retire, and any plan that promises freedom inside a year should be checked against that rate. A $997 course spends 520 evening hours in year one, $13,000 at $25 an hour, and hands back a study habit unless the math underneath it was already sound.

The hourly rate on a $300 bank bonus is the same ledger at small scale. A bonus that takes 45 minutes of work pays $400 an hour, and $400 an hour is a working wage in any town. The rate is honest because the hours are counted. Rate honesty scales: the same counting applied to a $997 course year-one comes out near $2 an hour, and the $4.90 hour you retire on was bought by years nobody framed.

The withdrawal math and the hourly-rate method live on Firenomics, the FIRE guides site that counts hours like dollars.
